# 7. Military & War

Military strategy models provide frameworks for competition, conflict, and
decision-making under extreme pressure.

---

## 7.1 Seeing the Front

**Core idea:** Leaders must personally observe the front before making
decisions. Don't rely solely on advisors, maps, and reports — they can all
be faulty or biased.

**Explanation:** Secondhand information is filtered through incentives and
perspective. Advisors may tell you what they think you want to hear, or what
serves their interests. Reports may be outdated or incomplete. Seeing the
front provides firsthand data and improves the quality of all subsequent
information (people know you might verify things yourself). This applies far
beyond the military: executives who talk to customers, managers who do the
work alongside their team, and policymakers who visit affected communities
all make better decisions.

**Pedagogical use:** Discuss the limitations of reports and dashboards. Apply
to management (MBWA — management by walking around), product development
(user research), and journalism (embedded reporting).

**Writing application:** Do primary research. Visit the places you write about.
Talk to the people you write about. Firsthand observation beats desk research
every time. Readers can tell the difference.

---

## 7.2 Asymmetric Warfare

**Core idea:** One side plays by different rules. The weaker party uses
unconventional tactics because they can't match the stronger party in
conventional strength.

**Explanation:** In asymmetric warfare, the weaker party can't out-muscle the
stronger one, so they use other tactics — terrorism, guerrilla warfare,
cyber-attacks — creating fear or damage disproportionate to their actual
destructive ability. The same dynamic appears in business: startups disrupt
incumbents by playing a different game rather than competing on the
incumbent's terms. Instead of building a better horse carriage, build a car.
Instead of better newspapers, build the internet.

**Pedagogical use:** Discuss asymmetric competition in business (disruption),
politics (grassroots vs. establishment), law (David vs. Goliath cases), and
personal development. Ask: "What game are we playing? Can we change the game?"

**Writing application:** Use asymmetric angles — approach a topic from an
unexpected direction that your competitors can't or won't. Find the angle
they're ignoring and own it.

---

## 7.3 Two-Front War

**Core idea:** Fighting on two fronts divides strength. Avoid multiple
conflicts simultaneously. Force opponents to split their attention.

**Explanation:** When Germany fought both Russia and the Allies in WWII, their
forces were split and weakened on both fronts. The same applies to
organizations: internal political battles distract from external competition.
A company fighting a price war while also battling a PR crisis is at a
significant disadvantage. Focus your energy on one front at a time. If you
can force your competitor to fight on multiple fronts, you've already won
half the battle.

**Pedagogical use:** Discuss resource allocation, strategic focus, and
prioritization. Apply to startups vs. incumbents, political campaigns, and
personal productivity (avoiding "context switching" as a two-front war on
attention).

**Writing application:** Simplify your argument to one main front. Don't fight
multiple battles in a single piece. If you have multiple points, pick the one
that matters most and go deep.

---

## 7.4 Counterinsurgency

**Core idea:** Conventional force often fails against insurgency. Success
requires a different approach — winning hearts and minds, not just battles.

**Explanation:** General Petraeus demonstrated that counterinsurgency involves
more than military force — it requires understanding the local population,
building trust, providing security, and addressing root causes. Tit-for-tat
escalation creates a destructive feedback loop that neither side can win.
Breaking the cycle requires patience, empathy, and a fundamentally different
strategy. The same applies to any protracted conflict — whether in business,
relationships, or politics.

**Pedagogical use:** Apply to conflict resolution, customer complaints,
organizational change, and political polarization. Discuss when force
escalates rather than solves. Ask: "What would a counterinsurgency approach
look like here?"

**Writing application:** Address objections not by overpowering them but by
understanding and reframing them. Don't attack the reader's position —
understand why they hold it first.

---

## 7.5 Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD)

**Core idea:** When two opponents are strong enough to destroy each other, the
conflict may stabilize. But stability comes with catastrophic tail risk.

**Explanation:** MAD is paradoxical: the stronger both sides become, the less
likely they are to attack — because any attack guarantees mutual annihilation.
This applies beyond nuclear weapons: price wars that would destroy both
companies, legal battles that would bankrupt both parties, personal arguments
that would destroy a relationship. However, in a fat-tailed world, MAD doesn't
eliminate risk — it pushes destruction into the tail of the distribution,
making accidents potentially catastrophic rather than merely damaging.

**Pedagogical use:** Discuss Cold War strategy, nuclear deterrence, and
escalation dynamics. Apply to competitive strategy (avoiding mutually
destructive competition), negotiation (BATNA), and relationship conflict.

**Writing application:** Use MAD as a framework for understanding stalemates,
deterrence, and high-stakes competition. Show when competition is productive
and when it risks mutual destruction.
